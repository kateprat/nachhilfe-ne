15.1 · Measuring angles: radians

What you need for E1:

Instead of degrees, you can measure an angle as the length of the arc on the unit circle (radius 1). The whole circle has circumference \(2\pi\), so:

Degrees and radians15.1.3$$360^\circ = 2\pi, \qquad 180^\circ = \pi, \qquad 90^\circ = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$$

That's all you need to memorise. Work everything else out from \(180^\circ = \pi\): divide by 180, multiply by π (or the other way round).

Try it yourself: what is 60° in radians?

\(60^\circ = \frac{60}{180}\pi = \frac{\pi}{3}\)

Every exercise works the same way: read the task and first think about how you would start. Then click through the solution with “Next step”, one step at a time. Before each click, think about what comes next. If you get stuck, “Need help?” at the top of the exercise takes you to the matching explanation. Anything marked ★ is not in the script: extra help. At the end of each section you can open “More from the script” if you want everything with the script's numbers.

E1

Need help? Degrees and radians ↑

(a) Convert the angles 0°, 360°, 180°, 90°, 60°, 30°, −90°, 45°, 1° exactly into radians, and \(1\) rad into degrees.
(b) Find the conversion functions \(d(r)\) (radians → degrees) and \(r(d)\) (degrees → radians).
  1. STEP 1

    The one thing you need to know

    Half a circle: half the circumference of the unit circle is π.

    $$180^\circ = \pi$$
  2. STEP 2

    Degrees → radians: divide by 180, multiply by π

    How many “180°” is it? That many π.

    $$\begin{aligned} 360^\circ &= 2\pi & 90^\circ &= \tfrac{90}{180}\pi = \tfrac{\pi}{2} & 60^\circ &= \tfrac{\pi}{3} \\ 30^\circ &= \tfrac{\pi}{6} & -90^\circ &= -\tfrac{\pi}{2} & 45^\circ &= \tfrac{\pi}{4} \\ 1^\circ &= \tfrac{\pi}{180} & 0^\circ &= 0 \end{aligned}$$
  3. STEP 3

    Radians → degrees: divide by π, multiply by 180

    $$1 = \tfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ$$
  4. STEP 4

    (b) Write it as functions

    The calculations from steps 2 and 3, just with a letter instead of a number: r = angle in radians, d = angle in degrees.

    $$d(r) = \frac{180}{\pi}\cdot r, \qquad r(d) = \frac{\pi}{180}\cdot d$$

    Check: \(d(\pi) = 180\) ✓, \(r(180) = \pi\) ✓

Only remember \(180^\circ = \pi\). Work out the rest each time.

Definition 15.1.2: radian

Draw a circle with any radius r around the vertex of the angle. The angle cuts out an arc of length b. Then

$$\alpha = \frac{b}{r}\ \text{rad}$$

This doesn't depend on r: a circle twice as big has an arc twice as long, so the fraction stays the same. That's why you may just as well use the unit circle (r = 1): then the angle is simply the arc length (Memo 15.1.9). You may leave out “rad”, 1 rad = 1. Calculators and programs (e.g. Python) almost always work in radians.

Corollary 15.1.7: arc length and sector area

In a circle with radius r, the angle α (in radians) gives

$$b = \alpha\cdot r, \qquad A = \frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot r^2$$

Check with the full circle, \(\alpha = 2\pi\): \(b = 2\pi r\) (circumference) ✓, \(A = \pi r^2\) ✓

15.2 · Trigonometric functions

What you need for E2–E7:

15.2.2Turn a ray by the angle α (anticlockwise, starting from the positive x-axis). It meets the circle at the point \(P_\alpha\). Then:

  • cos(α) = the x-coordinate of P (how far right/left)
  • sin(α) = the y-coordinate of P (how far up/down)
  • tan(α) = the slope of the ray, i.e. \(\frac{\sin(\alpha)}{\cos(\alpha)}\). Exactly the m from \(\m x + \q\)! You can see it on the line \(x = 1\).

Two things you can see straight from the picture:

  • cos and sin are always between −1 and 1. P is on a circle of radius 1, after all.
  • Pythagoras15.2.6: the triangle made of cos, sin and radius 1 is right-angled, so \(\sin^2(\alpha) + \cos^2(\alpha) = 1\).

Need help? The recipes ↑

Draw a unit circle with a diameter of 8 cm. (a)–(c) Mark \(P_{55^\circ}, P_{290^\circ}, P_{-190^\circ}, P_{380^\circ}\), measure cos, sin and tan and check with the calculator. (d)–(h) For which α is \(\sin(\alpha) = 0.8\), \(\cos(\alpha) = -0.2\), \(\tan(\alpha) = -2\), \(\sin(\alpha) = 4.2\), \(\cos(\alpha) = -2\)?
  1. TIP

    Measure in units, not in cm

    8 cm diameter = 4 cm radius = 1 unit. So divide the measured cm by 4. In the unit circle above you can set any angle and compare.

  2. (a)(b)

    Read off cos = x, sin = y

    Careful: 380° = 360° + 20°, the same point as 20°. −190° means: 190° clockwise.

    $$\begin{aligned} \cos 55^\circ &\approx 0.574, & \sin 55^\circ &\approx 0.819 \\ \cos 290^\circ &\approx 0.342, & \sin 290^\circ &\approx -0.940 \\ \cos(-190^\circ) &\approx -0.985, & \sin(-190^\circ) &\approx 0.174 \\ \cos 380^\circ &\approx 0.940, & \sin 380^\circ &\approx 0.342 \end{aligned}$$
  3. (c)

    Read off tan on the line x = 1

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 55^\circ &\approx 1.428 & \tan 290^\circ &\approx -2.747 \\ \tan(-190^\circ) &\approx -0.176 & \tan 380^\circ &\approx 0.364 \end{aligned}$$
  4. (d)

    \(\sin(\alpha) = 0.8\)

    Draw the horizontal line y = 0.8. It meets the circle twice, mirrored left and right.

    $$\alpha_1 \approx 53.1^\circ, \quad \alpha_2 = 180^\circ - 53.1^\circ \approx 126.9^\circ, \qquad \alpha = \alpha_{1,2} + k\cdot 360^\circ,\ k \in \mathbb{Z}$$

    \(k\cdot 360^\circ\): every full turn more or less lands on the same point again. That's why there are infinitely many solutions.

  5. (e)

    \(\cos(\alpha) = -0.2\)

    Vertical line x = −0.2. The two points are mirrored top and bottom.

    $$\alpha \approx \pm 101.5^\circ + k\cdot 360^\circ \quad(\text{so also } 258.5^\circ)$$
  6. (f)

    \(\tan(\alpha) = -2\)

    Line through O with slope −2. It meets the circle at two opposite points, 180° apart.

    $$\alpha \approx 116.6^\circ + k\cdot 180^\circ$$

    The calculator gives \(-63.4^\circ\): the same family (\(-63.4^\circ + 180^\circ = 116.6^\circ\)).

  7. (g)(h)

    \(\sin(\alpha) = 4.2\), \(\cos(\alpha) = -2\)

    No solution: cos and sin are always between −1 and 1.

    45°: half a square

    At 45°, x and y are the same length. Pythagoras: \(x^2 + x^2 = 1\).

    60°: like 30°, with x and y swapped

E6

Need help? cos, sin, tan on the unit circle ↑

(a) Which of the values \(\pm\sin(\pm\alpha)\), \(\pm\cos(\pm\alpha)\) are equal?
(b) Which of \(\sin\alpha\), \(\sin(\alpha + 90^\circ)\), \(-\sin(\alpha + 180^\circ)\), \(\sin(\alpha + 360^\circ)\), \(\cos\alpha\), \(-\cos(\alpha + 90^\circ)\), \(-\cos(\alpha + 180^\circ)\), \(\cos(\alpha + 360^\circ)\) are equal?
(c) What about \(\tan\alpha\), \(\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha\), 1, \(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}\)?
  1. TIP

    Draw, don't calculate

    Draw \(P_\alpha\) and the turned or mirrored point. Then compare x and y. In the unit circle above you can set e.g. 30° and then −30°.

  2. (a)

    −α: mirrored in the x-axis

    x stays the same, y changes sign.

    $$\cos(-\alpha) = \cos(\alpha), \qquad \sin(-\alpha) = -\sin(\alpha)$$
  3. (b)

    Turn by +90°, +180°, +360°

    +360°: same point. +180°: the opposite point, both coordinates change sign. +90°: (x, y) becomes (−y, x).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin\alpha &= -\sin(\alpha + 180^\circ) \\ &= \sin(\alpha + 360^\circ) \\ &= -\cos(\alpha + 90^\circ) \\[6pt] \cos\alpha &= \sin(\alpha + 90^\circ) \\ &= -\cos(\alpha + 180^\circ) \\ &= \cos(\alpha + 360^\circ) \end{aligned}$$
  4. (c)

    Two pairs

    $$\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha} \quad(\text{definition}), \qquad \sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1 \quad(\text{Pythagoras})$$

E7

Need help? Recipe 6 ↑

If you know one of the numbers \(\cos\alpha\), \(\sin\alpha\), \(\tan\alpha\), you can find the other two up to their sign. Use \(\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha}\) and \(\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1\).
(a) cos known,
(b) sin known,
(c) tan known.
  1. (a)

    cos known → sin

    Solve Pythagoras for \(\sin\). The square root gives two possibilities: ±.

    $$\sin^2\alpha = 1 - \cos^2\alpha \iff \sin\alpha = \pm\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\alpha}$$
  2. (a)

    cos known → tan

    $$\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\alpha}}{\cos\alpha}$$
  3. (a)

    Signs for \(\alpha \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)\)

    That's 90° to 180°, so quadrant II: sin positive, cos negative, tan negative.

    $$\sin\alpha = +\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\alpha}, \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\alpha}}{\cos\alpha}\ (\text{negative, because } \cos\alpha \lt 0)$$

    Example: \(\cos\alpha = -\frac35\) → \(\sin\alpha = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{25}} = \frac45\), \(\tan\alpha = \frac{4/5}{-3/5} = -\frac43\).

  4. (b)

    sin known

    Exactly the same, with sin and cos swapped.

    $$\cos\alpha = \pm\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\alpha}, \qquad \tan\alpha = \pm\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\alpha}}$$
  5. (c)

    tan known

    Trick: substitute \(\sin\alpha = \tan\alpha\cdot\cos\alpha\) into Pythagoras. Then only cos is left.

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan^2\alpha\cdot\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha &= 1 \\ \cos^2\alpha\,(\tan^2\alpha + 1) &= 1 \\ \cos\alpha &= \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2\alpha}} \\ \sin\alpha &= \tan\alpha\cdot\cos\alpha \\ &= \pm\frac{\tan\alpha}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2\alpha}} \end{aligned}$$

15.3 · Polar coordinates

What you need for E9–E11:

Script pages 7–9 are still missing hereThey contain the start of 15.3 (incl. 15.3.1) and exercise E8. As soon as we have them, they will be added here.

So far, a point was given by \(x\) and \(y\): “3 to the right, 1 up”. You can also describe it like a direction: “go 3 units, at an angle of 30°”. That's polar coordinates.

Polar coordinates15.3.2$$P = (r, \varphi)$$
  • r = radial coordinate = distance of P from the origin O (the pole). Always \(r \ge 0\).
  • φ (phi) = angle coordinate = the angle between the positive x-axis (the polar axis) and the ray from O through P. Usually \(0^\circ \le \varphi \lt 360^\circ\). For the origin itself (r = 0) you take φ = 0°.

Careful: \((3, 30^\circ)\) in polar coordinates and \((3, 30)\) in cartesian coordinates are completely different points. The ° sign (or π) tells you it's an angle.

The link to the unit circle: on the unit circle (\(r = 1\)) the point is \((\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\). A circle with radius \(r\) is the unit circle stretched by \(r\), so multiply both coordinates by r.

  1. Polar → cartesian15.3.4\(x = r\cdot\cos(\varphi), \quad y = r\cdot\sin(\varphi)\)Works for every angle, also negative ones and angles of 360° or more.
  2. Cartesian → polar ★\(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\)Pythagoras: x and y are the legs, r is the hypotenuse. For φ: sketch the point, find the angle to the x-axis (reference angle) from \(\tan = \frac{|y|}{|x|}\), then adjust for the quadrant (table below).
P lies in quadrantI (right, top)II (left, top)III (left, bottom)IV (right, bottom)
φ =reference angle180° − ref.180° + ref.360° − ref.
Why not just use the calculator's tan⁻¹?\(\tan^{-1}\) only gives angles between −90° and 90°. It can't tell \((1, 1)\) and \((-1, -1)\) apart: both have \(\frac{y}{x} = 1\). That's why you always sketch first.

    P, Q, R and S are mirror images of each other. Once you have P, the others are quick.

    \(Q = (-1, \sqrt3)\)

    \(R = (1, -\sqrt3)\)

    Right and bottom: quadrant IV.

    $$r = 2, \qquad \varphi = 360^\circ - 60^\circ = 300^\circ \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad R = (2,\ 300^\circ)$$
  7. S

    \(S = (-1, -\sqrt3)\)

    Left and bottom: quadrant III. S is exactly opposite P.

    $$r = 2, \qquad \varphi = 180^\circ + 60^\circ = 240^\circ \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad S = (2,\ 240^\circ)$$
r with Pythagoras. φ: sketch → reference angle → adjust for the quadrant.

Rotating points

A point turns around O at constant speed, like a mark on a wheel. Its distance r stays the same, only the angle changes. So: find the angle at time t, then use the polar → cartesian formula.

$$\varphi(t) = \omega\cdot t + \varphi_0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P(t) = \begin{pmatrix} r\cdot\cos(\omega t + \varphi_0) \\ r\cdot\sin(\omega t + \varphi_0) \end{pmatrix}$$
  • r = radius (the number in front of cos and sin)
  • ω (omega) = angle per second, in radians (the number in front of t). One full turn is 2π in T seconds, so \(\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}\) and \(T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}\).
  • φ₀ = angle at the start (t = 0)
  • Sign of ω: positive → anticlockwise (the normal direction), negative → clockwise

15.3.5Special case: unit circle, start at \((1, 0)\), speed 1 unit per second. Then the angle is simply t, and the point is \((\cos t, \sin t)\).

A wheel with its axis at the origin turns at constant angular velocity. A point A is marked at distance r from the origin.
(a) Find \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) for \(r = 7\), period \(T = 20\) s, start \(A(0) = (r, 0)\).
(b) Find \(A(t)\) for \(r = 1\), \(T = 2\pi\), \(A(0) = (r, 0)\). How fast is the point moving along the circle?
(c) Find \(A(t)\) for \(r = 1\), \(T = 2\pi\), \(A(0) = (0, r)\).
(d) \(B(t) = \left(3\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}t\right),\ 3\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}t\right)\right)\). Distance r? Period T? Where is B at t = 0?
(e) \(B(t) = \left(\cos\left(-5t + \frac{\pi}{3}\right),\ \sin\left(-5t + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right)\). Distance r? Period T? Where is B at t = 0? In which direction does it turn?
  1. STEP 1

    (a) How fast does the angle grow?

    One full turn (2π) takes 20 seconds. So per second, the wheel turns a 20th of that.

    $$\varphi(t) = \frac{2\pi}{20}\cdot t = \frac{\pi}{10}\cdot t$$

    Check: \(t = 0\) → angle 0 ✓ (start on the x-axis). \(t = 20\) → angle \(2\pi\) ✓ (one full turn).

  2. STEP 2

    (a) Polar → cartesian

    r = 7, angle φ(t). Use x = r·cos φ, y = r·sin φ.

    $$x(t) = 7\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{10}t\right), \qquad y(t) = 7\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{10}t\right)$$

    Test: after 5 s (a quarter of 20 s) the point should be at the top: \(A(5) = (7\cos\frac{\pi}{2},\ 7\sin\frac{\pi}{2}) = (0, 7)\) ✓

  3. (b)

    T = 2π: the angle is simply t

    \(\varphi(t) = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi}\cdot t = t\). Speed = distance ÷ time: one lap is the circumference \(2\pi\cdot 1\), and it takes \(2\pi\) seconds.

    $$A(t) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos t \\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v = \frac{2\pi}{2\pi} = 1\ \tfrac{\text{unit}}{\text{second}}$$

    This is exactly Memo 15.3.5.

  4. (c)

    Start at the top: the angle is π/2 ahead

    (0, 1) is at 90° = π/2. Same speed as in (b), but the angle starts at π/2 instead of 0.

    $$\varphi(t) = t + \tfrac{\pi}{2} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A(t) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\left(t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \\ \sin\left(t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin t \\ \cos t\end{pmatrix}$$

    The short form comes from E6 (b). Check: \(A(0) = (-\sin 0, \cos 0) = (0, 1)\) ✓

  5. (d)

    Read off r, ω, start

    Compare with \(r\cos(\omega t + \varphi_0)\): in front 3, before t \(\frac{\pi}{3}\), nothing added.

    $$r = 3, \qquad \omega = \tfrac{\pi}{3} \Rightarrow T = 2\pi : \tfrac{\pi}{3} = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac{3}{\pi} = 6\text{ s}, \qquad B(0) = (3\cos 0,\ 3\sin 0) = (3, 0)$$

  6. The angle is −5t + π/3. In which direction does B turn?

    (e)

    Read off r, ω, start, direction

    No number in front of cos and sin means r = 1. The angle changes by −5 per second: it gets smaller, so the point turns backwards (clockwise).

    $$r = 1, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{|-5|} = \frac{2\pi}{5} \approx 1.26\text{ s}, \qquad B(0) = \left(\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3},\ \sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \left(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$$

    Direction: clockwise, because of the minus sign in front of t.

\(\big(r\cos(\omega t + \varphi_0),\ r\sin(\omega t + \varphi_0)\big)\): r = radius, \(T = \frac{2\pi}{|\omega|}\), φ₀ = start angle, sign of ω = direction.

15.4 · Trigonometry in right triangles

What you need for triangles:

Trigonometry means “measuring triangles”. In a right triangle, sin, cos and tan are simply ratios of two sides.

15.4.2First the names. They always depend on which angle you are looking at:

  • Hypotenuse: opposite the right angle, the longest side. Always the same.
  • Adjacent side (Ankathete): the leg that touches α.
  • Opposite side (Gegenkathete): the leg across from α.

Look at the other acute angle instead, and adjacent and opposite swap roles.

sin, cos and tan in a right triangle15.4.3

For each of the two acute angles α:

$$\sin(\alpha) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos(\alpha) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \tan(\alpha) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$$

Memory aid: SOH CAH TOA (Sin = O/H, Cos = A/H, Tan = O/A).

Try it yourself: a right triangle has legs 3 and 4 and hypotenuse 5. α is the angle opposite the side 3. What are sin α, cos α and tan α?

\(\sin\alpha = \frac35\), \(\cos\alpha = \frac45\), \(\tan\alpha = \frac34\)

Link to polar coordinates\(x = r\cos\varphi\) is just “adjacent = hypotenuse · cos”: x is the adjacent side, r the hypotenuse. Same idea, two names.

Proof of Theorem 15.4.3
  1. The triangle from the unit circleTake the point \(P_\alpha\) on the unit circle and drop a vertical line to the x-axis. This gives a right triangle (red): hypotenuse 1 (the radius), adjacent side cos α, opposite side sin α. That is just the definition 15.2.2.
  2. It is similar to the triangle of the theoremBoth triangles have the angle α and a right angle, so the third angle is the same too (angles add up to 180°). Same angles means the triangles are similar (ähnlich): one is a stretched copy of the other.
  3. Stretch factor cCall the hypotenuse of the theorem's triangle c. The red triangle has hypotenuse 1, so you stretch it by the factor c. Every side gets multiplied by c: $$\text{hypotenuse} = c, \qquad \text{adjacent} = c\cdot\cos\alpha, \qquad \text{opposite} = c\cdot\sin\alpha$$
  4. Divide$$\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{c\cdot\sin\alpha}{c} = \sin\alpha, \qquad \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = \frac{c\cdot\cos\alpha}{c} = \cos\alpha$$$$\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{c\cdot\sin\alpha}{c\cdot\cos\alpha} = \frac{\sin\alpha}{\cos\alpha} = \tan\alpha \qquad \square$$
